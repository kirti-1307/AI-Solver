Standard reaction thermodynamics

Pick a reaction or type your own. The elemental balance is checked before anything is calculated. Write phases as (g), (l), (s); if you leave the phase out, gas is assumed when available.

Effect of pressure at constant temperature

For an ideal gas, enthalpy does not depend on pressure. For a real gas it does, and the difference is the residual enthalpy HR. Here every reactant and product is taken as the pure species at T and P: ΔH(T,P) = ΔH°(T) + Σ νi HiR(T,P).

Build a reaction from other reactions (Hess's law)

Add two or more reactions, each with a multiplier. A negative multiplier reverses the reaction. The solver adds the stoichiometry, cancels the intermediates and gets ΔH, ΔS, ΔG and K of the net reaction two ways: by summing the steps and directly from the net equation.

Product slate: what actually forms? extra feature 1

A single-reaction ΔG tells you whether one reaction can go. A real reactor has several reactions competing for the same atoms. Here you only choose which species are allowed to exist; the solver minimises the total Gibbs energy subject to the element balances and returns the equilibrium mixture. No reaction list is needed. Drag a slider and the mixture follows.

Heat lab: how hot does it get, and how much heat is needed? extra feature 2

An energy balance around the reactor: Q = ΔHR + ΔH°298 + ΔHP (feed to 298.15 K, reaction there, products to the outlet). With Q = 0 it gives the flame temperature. The second view is the bomb calorimeter of the lab, where the vessel is closed and the heat released is ΔU.

Solver versus published research

Data: Smith, Van Ness & Abbott, Introduction to Chemical Engineering Thermodynamics: Table C.1 (gas heat capacities), C.2 (solids), C.3 (liquids), C.4 (formation properties at 298.15 K) and B.1 (critical constants). Hydrogen peroxide and acetic acid, which SVN does not fully cover, use the NIST Chemistry WebBook and are labelled wherever they appear. Standard state: pure species at 1 bar; gases as ideal gases. R = 8.314 J/(mol·K). Air is taken as 21 mol-% O2 and 79 mol-% N2.

All calculations run in your browser. The solver is the Python package thermosolver, executed with Pyodide.
