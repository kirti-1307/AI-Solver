Standard reaction thermodynamics

Pick a reaction or type your own. The elemental balance is checked before anything is calculated. Write phases as (g), (l), (s); if you leave the phase out, gas is assumed when available. The standard state (the ° sign) fixes the pressure at 1 bar; the temperature is yours to choose, from 200 K upwards for gases.

Ideal gas against real gas: what pressure does to the heat of reaction

For an ideal gas, enthalpy does not depend on pressure. For a real gas it does. The difference between the two is the residual enthalpy, HR = H(real gas) − H(ideal gas), which comes from an equation of state. Here every reactant and product is taken as the pure species at T and P, at constant temperature: ΔH(T,P) = ΔH°(T) + Σ νi HiR(T,P).

Build a reaction from other reactions (Hess's law)

Add two or more reactions, each with a multiplier. A negative multiplier reverses the reaction. The solver adds the stoichiometry, cancels the intermediates and gets ΔH, ΔS, ΔG and K of the net reaction two ways: by summing the steps and directly from the net equation.

Product slate: what actually forms? extra feature 1

Put the feed gases in a reactor and wait until nothing changes: what mixture comes out? One ΔG cannot tell, because several reactions compete for the same atoms. Here you only say which species are allowed to exist. The solver then finds the mixture with the lowest total Gibbs energy that keeps every atom, so no reaction list is needed. Drag a slider and the mixture follows.

Heat lab: how hot does it get, and how much heat is needed? extra feature 2

An energy balance around the reactor: Q = ΔHR + ΔH°298 + ΔHP (feed to 298.15 K, reaction there, products to the outlet). With Q = 0 it gives the flame temperature. If the outlet is cooled below its dew point, part of the water condenses and its latent heat is added as a fourth step. The second view is the bomb calorimeter of the lab, where the vessel is closed and the heat released is ΔU.

Data: Smith, Van Ness & Abbott, Introduction to Chemical Engineering Thermodynamics: Table C.1 (gas heat capacities), C.2 (solids), C.3 (liquids), C.4 (formation properties at 298.15 K) and B.1 (critical constants). Hydrogen peroxide and acetic acid, which SVN does not fully cover, use the NIST Chemistry WebBook and are labelled wherever they appear. SVN's heat capacities start at 298 K; from 200 K to 298.15 K they come from the NIST-JANAF tables and the NIST Chemistry WebBook. Standard state: the pure species at 1 bar, at whatever temperature is chosen; gases as ideal gases. 298.15 K is only the reference temperature of the formation data. R = 8.314 J/(mol·K). Air is taken as 21 mol-% O2 and 79 mol-% N2.

All calculations run in your browser. The solver is the Python package thermosolver, executed with Pyodide.
